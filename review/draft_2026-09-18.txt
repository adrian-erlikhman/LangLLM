Do LLM Fingerprints Survive Outside English?
Interpretable Attribution of Five Frontier Models
Across Seven Languages

Abstract—Tools that detect or attribute AI-generated text are
built and evaluated mostly in English, while the settings where
attribution matters, such as disinformation tracing and content
moderation, are multilingual. We ask whether the stylistic
fingerprint of a specific large language model (LLM) survives
outside English. We collected 840 persuasive essays from five
frontier models (GPT-5.5, Gemini 3.5 Flash, Claude Opus 4.7,
Grok 4.3, DeepSeek V4 Pro) in seven languages ordered by
training-data resource level, holding content fixed with natively
authored prompts. Each text is described by 21 interpretable
features defined on Universal Dependencies, so each feature
has the same meaning in every language. A logistic-regression
classifier attributes texts five ways at 59–72% accuracy in every
language (chance 20%, leave-one-prompt-out), and accuracy
does not decline with resource level ( p = 0.36). Language
explains far more feature variance than model identity (mean
partial η2 0.54 vs. 0.15), yet a classifier trained on one language
beats chance on all 42 cross-language pairs. Models become
more alike as resources fall (centroid separation vs. resource
rank, ρ = −0.96) without becoming indistinguishable. Machine
translation does not remove the fingerprint: attribution of
translated text stays at 57–70%, because paragraphing and
sentence rhythm survive translation while vocabulary does not.
The five models themselves, asked which model wrote each text,
score 20–25%. Code, prompts, and all texts are released.
Index Terms—authorship attribution, AI-generated text, multi-
lingual text mining, stylometry, large language models
1. Introduction
Most work on machine-generated text asks whether a text
was written by a person or by a model. A second question
matters for provenance: which model wrote it. Answering it
lets an analyst link a coordinated influence campaign to a
toolchain, check a vendor’s claim about where its content
came from, or apply platform rules that differ by provider.
The attribution literature that exists is almost entirely English
and mostly relies on neural embeddings whose decisions
cannot be inspected [1], [2], [3].
There are two reasons to doubt that English results carry
over to other languages. First, models see far less training
text in lower-resource languages [ 4], so their writing there
may be less distinctive or less stable. Second, languages
differ in script and morphology, so many classic stylometric
measures (readability formulas, contractions, hedge lists) are
undefined outside English, and the measures that are defined
may track the language more than the author.
We address both problems with a design that holds
content fixed across languages and describes every text with
features defined on Universal Dependencies (UD) [ 5]. We
ask six questions. RQ1: Can five frontier models be told
apart within each of seven languages? RQ2: Does attribution
weaken as a language’s resource level falls? RQ3: Is a
model’s fingerprint shared across languages or specific to
each? RQ4: Do models converge on one style in lower-
resource languages? RQ5: Does machine translation erase the
fingerprint? RQ6: As a baseline, can the models themselves
identify the author?
Our contributions are:
• A content-controlled multilingual corpus of 840
essays (5 models × 7 languages × 12 prompts × 2
generations) and 1,440 machine translations, released
with code.
• A 21-feature interpretable representation that means
the same thing in every language, with which five-
way attribution reaches 59–72% in all seven lan-
guages and shows no significant resource gradient.
• Evidence that the fingerprint is largely language-
invariant while models still converge stylistically in
lower-resource languages, and an account of why
both hold.
• Evidence that translation does not launder the fin-
gerprint and that LLM judges attribute at or near
chance.
All analyses and decision rules were written down before
data collection; deviations are noted where they occur.

2. Related Work
Detection. Zero-shot detectors such as DetectGPT [ 6],
Fast-DetectGPT [7] and Binoculars [8], and supervised detec-
tors such as Ghostbuster [ 9], separate human from machine
text. Multilingual benchmarks, including MULTITuDE [ 10],
M4 [11] and SemEval-2024 Task 8 [ 12], show that detection
degrades outside English [ 13]. These works address human
versus machine, not which machine.
Model attribution. Uchendu et al. framed attribution
among neural text generators [ 1] and built the TuringBench
benchmark [ 2], both in English. Sun et al. showed that
embedding-based classifiers separate outputs of current
commercial model families with high accuracy [ 3], again
in English and with opaque features. LLMmap identifies a
model actively through probe queries [ 14], which requires
access to the model rather than only its text. Classic stylom-
etry relies on function words and character n-grams that are
tied to a language [ 15]; stylometric detection of AI text has
so far been applied to English [ 16]. To our knowledge, no
prior study performs passive, interpretable model attribution
across a resource gradient.
Homogenization and LLM judges. Writing with LLMs
reduces content diversity [ 17], [18], and LLM-style markers
are rising in real text [ 19]. These studies compare people
with models in English; whether models converge on each
other where training data is thin has not been tested. LLM
evaluators can recognize and favor their own outputs [ 20];
we use LLM judges only as a baseline, and a companion
study [21] examines self-recognition in English.
3. Data and Method
3.1. Models and languages
The five subject models, queried through OpenRouter
with the served model string logged for every response, are
GPT-5.5, Gemini 3.5 Flash, Claude Opus 4.7, Grok 4.3
and DeepSeek V4 Pro. The seven languages are English,
Spanish, Chinese, Russian, Japanese, Turkish and Hindi,
assigned ordinal resource ranks 1–7 in that order to reflect
their approximate share of web training data [ 4]. We use an
ordinal rank because published numeric estimates disagree
by source.
3.2. Content-matched prompts
Twelve English schemas each fix a topic, a stance (six for,
six against), three supporting claims, a prose-only instruction
and a reading-level tier (four general, four educated, four
expert). For each language, a model that is not a subject
(Llama 4 Maverick) wrote a native prompt from the schema
rather than translating an English prompt, so the wording is
idiomatic while the content is fixed. A model from a third
family (Qwen 3.7 Plus) then read each of the 84 prompts
blind, extracted the topic, stance and claims, and compared
them with the schema; prompts that failed were regenerated
with the reviewer’s note until all 84 passed.
3.3. Generation and validation
Each prompt was sent as a single user turn with no
system prompt at temperature 0.7, twice per cell with fixed
seeds, with reasoning traces excluded from the output, giving
5 × 12 × 7 × 2 = 840 responses. Each response passed
language identification (lingua, confidence ≥ 0.6), a length
window of 150–900 word-equivalents (characters converted
for Chinese and Japanese), and a per-language refusal check.
One Grok response to a Hindi prompt was written in English
and excluded, leaving 839 texts. Ten DeepSeek responses
exhausted a 4,000-token budget on hidden reasoning and
were re-collected at 16,000 tokens.
3.4. Features
Every text was parsed with Stanza [ 22] (tokenization,
part of speech, lemma and dependency parse) in all seven
languages; Chinese and Japanese were pre-split on sentence-
final punctuation. We compute 21 features in five groups.
Lexical: moving-average type-token ratio (MATTR, window
50) [23], hapax rate, mean token length, Zipf slope. Syntactic:
mean and SD of sentence length, burstiness [ 24], mean
maximum dependency depth, subordinate-clause rate ( acl,
advcl, ccomp, xcomp, csubj per sentence), function-
word ratio, first-person rate. Structure: paragraph count,
paragraph length, question rate, connective rate. Punctuation:
commas, colons, dashes and semicolons per 1,000 tokens,
with full-width and script variants mapped to the same feature.
Character: character-bigram entropy and digit rate. English-
only measures such as readability formulas, contractions,
hedges and passive rate are excluded.
3.5. Analysis
The unit of analysis is one response. The two generations
of a prompt are not independent, so every cross-validation
split and every clustered standard error groups by prompt.
RQ1. Within each language, a multinomial logistic re-
gression on standardized features (primary, interpretable)
and a 300-tree random forest (ceiling), evaluated with leave-
one-prompt-out cross-validation (12 folds), bootstrap 95%
confidence intervals, and an exact binomial test against
0.20. RQ2. A binomial GLM of cell-level correctness on
resource rank with prompt-clustered standard errors, plus
Spearman’s ρ over the seven per-language accuracies; as a
robustness check, features residualized on log length within
each language. RQ3. For each feature, a two-way ANOV A
(model × language, prompt as a blocking factor) with partial
η2; and a transfer matrix in which features are z-scored
within each language, a classifier is trained on language A
and tested on language B. RQ4. After z-scoring within each
language, the mean pairwise Euclidean distance between
the five model centroids, the silhouette of model labels and
the between/within scatter ratio, with stratified bootstrap
intervals and Spearman’s ρ against rank. RQ5. All 120
English responses were translated into the six other languages
by Google Translate and by a free open model that is not

4. Results
4.1. RQ1: Attribution works in every language
Table 1 and Fig. 1 show five-way accuracy of 0.59–0.72
in every language, with every confidence interval excluding
chance (all p <10−20). Per-model recall is highest for GPT-
5.5 (67–92% across languages) and Grok 4.3 (63–88%),
followed by Claude Opus 4.7 (63–79%) and Gemini 3.5 Flash
(54–75%). DeepSeek V4 Pro is the hardest to identify (25–
58%); it is most often mistaken for Claude in English and for
GPT or Gemini in Hindi. By mean absolute coefficient, the
fingerprint is carried mainly by paragraph count and length,
comma and colon rates, and lexical diversity (hapax rate, Zipf
slope, bigram entropy); dependency depth, subordination and
question rate contribute little.
Length is part of the signal: Grok writes the shortest
responses in all seven languages, and GPT-5.5 the longest in

4.2. RQ2: No resource gradient
The cell-level GLM gives β = −0.038 log-odds per
rank step (SE 0.042, p = 0.36), less than one percentage
point of accuracy per step; with length-residualized features,
β = −0.053 (p = 0.16). Spearman’s ρ over the seven
languages is −0.45 (p = 0.31). The ordering is not monotone:
Japanese (rank 5) matches English, and Hindi (rank 7) is
more identifiable than Spanish and Turkish. Our pre-specified
criterion for a gradient (negative coefficient, p <0.05) is
not met.
4.3. RQ3: A shared fingerprint with a language
accent
Averaged over the 21 features, partial η2 is 0.54 for
language, 0.15 for model, 0.13 for the model × language
interaction and 0.16 for prompt. Language dominates every
feature tied to morphology or vocabulary (token length 0.99,
function-word ratio 0.94, hapax rate 0.93, Zipf slope 0.93,
MATTR 0.88), as expected when comparing Chinese char-
acters, Turkish agglutination and Hindi postpositions. Model
effects are largest for hapax rate (0.38), Zipf slope (0.37),
comma rate (0.32), bigram entropy (0.31) and paragraph
count (0.29), and smallest for subordination, question rate and
dependency depth (≤ 0.03). The models differ in how varied
their vocabulary is and how they punctuate and paragraph,
not in clause structure.

(Fig. 2). A classifier trained on any language reaches 0.37–

4.4. RQ4: Models converge where data is thin
All three separation measures fall with resource rank.
Mean centroid distance drops from 3.59 in English to 2.69
in Hindi ( ρ = −0.96, p <0.001), and the Hindi interval lies
entirely below the English one; the between/within scatter
ratio falls from 0.41 to 0.18 ( ρ = −1.00); and the silhouette
falls from 0.04 to −0.02 (ρ = −0.93, p = 0.003). Pairwise
distances locate the change. The largest English gaps, GPT–
Grok (5.69) and DeepSeek–Grok (4.86), shrink to 2.69 and
2.02 in Hindi, and Claude–Grok goes from 5.42 to 3.46.
Grok’s terse, lightly punctuated English register is the outlier
that disappears; the other four models were never far apart.
Convergence and stable attribution are compatible. Cen-
troid distance averages over all 21 features, most of which
converge, while a classifier needs only the few that keep
separating the models (paragraph count, comma and colon
rates, lexical-diversity slope). In lower-resource languages
the five models write more alike than they do in English,
but not identically.
4.5. RQ5: Translation does not launder the finger-
print
Attribution on translated text alone runs 0.57–0.70 (Ta-
ble 2), and every interval excludes chance ( p < 0.001).
Translating a text costs about nine points relative to the
English originals, roughly what moving from English to a
native lower-resource language costs. A classifier trained
on the English originals reads the translations at 0.66 (0.77
for the LLM’s Hindi), while one trained on the models’
own native-language responses reads them at only 0.52–0.53.
Translated GPT-5.5 text resembles English GPT-5.5 more
than it resembles native Spanish GPT-5.5: the translator
carries over the source’s structural habits instead of restyling
the text.

4.6. RQ6: LLM judges are near chance
Asked which of the five models wrote each text, the
judges score 20.3% (DeepSeek) to 25.4% (Claude) overall.
Only Claude is above chance ( p < 0.001), and its best
language is Hindi (36%). Each judge defaults to one answer:
GPT-5.5 names itself for 82% of texts regardless of author,
Claude most often names GPT-5.5, and the other three most
often name Claude. The feature classifier exceeds the best
judge by 28–48 points in every language (Table 1). This is
an instant-answer condition; judges allowed to reason may
do better.
5. Discussion
Three practical points follow. First, attribution with
interpretable features does not require English: accuracy
in Hindi and Turkish is within a few points of Spanish,
and a classifier trained in one language transfers to the
others. Second, translation is not an effective way to hide
which model wrote a text, because the features that carry
the fingerprint are structural, and a classifier trained on
English can be applied across a translation boundary without
retraining. Third, asking a model who wrote a text is not a
substitute for measurement.
The convergence result (RQ4) bears on the homoge-
nization literature [ 17], [18]. Model-generated text in lower-
resource languages is drifting toward a shared register faster

than in English. As more of the text in these languages is
machine-written, that register may become the default style
that readers and future training sets encounter.
Dual use. The same pipeline that attributes text to a
model can attribute text to a person, and our translation
result suggests that machine translation offers pseudonymous
human writers little protection against structural stylometry.
We release only model-generated text and no tools aimed at
human authors.
Limitations. (i) We study one genre, persuasive essays
with a three-claim structure; paragraph count partly reflects
how each model follows that instruction, and other genres
may fingerprint differently. (ii) Length is entangled with
style; the length-controlled result holds only non-linearly.
(iii) Resource level is an ordinal rank over seven languages.
(iv) Two generations per cell give intervals of about ±8
points. (v) Prompt fidelity was checked by a model rather
than by native speakers. (vi) Results describe model versions
served in September 2026, and fingerprints will drift with
updates. (vii) Judges were tested without extended reasoning.
6. Conclusion
Across seven languages and five frontier models, inter-
pretable UD-based features attribute text to its source model
at 59–72% accuracy with no significant decline in lower-
resource languages. The fingerprint is mostly shared across
languages, survives machine translation through structural
features, and is far easier to read with simple statistics
than by asking the models. At the same time, the models’
styles converge where training data is thin. Future work will
test other genres, human-verified prompts, numeric resource
covariates and reasoning-enabled judges.
